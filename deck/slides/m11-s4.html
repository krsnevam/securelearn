Real case · 2024, now cheaper

The CFO who was never on the call

What happened

An Arup employee in Hong Kong paid out about US$25 million after a video call in which the CFO and colleagues were all deepfakes. Voice and video cloning have since become cheaper and faster.

Your situation: Your new accounts-payable agent processes vendor emails. A “vendor CFO” emails a bank-change request, then calls to confirm in a familiar voice.

Your call · 15 min

  1. Which payment controls survive a perfect deepfake?
  2. Should the agent ever process bank-detail changes?
  3. What is our call-back rule, and who owns it?

Source: Arup statements and Hong Kong police briefings, February–May 2024

MODULE 11

Attack Surface & Risk Landscape

5 of 10

Concept tested: agent impersonation and human-agent trust exploitation (OWASP ASI09). Model answer. 1) Controls that do not rely on recognising a face or voice: call-back to a number already on file (never one given in the request), dual approval for bank changes, a cooling-off period before the first payment to new details, and payment limits. 2) No: bank-detail changes should be excluded from the agent's tools entirely and routed to a human process. 3) Treasury owns it; it applies even when the request appears to come from the CEO. Leadership point: when anyone can be faked, process beats recognition. Tie this to the capstone case "The vendor who wasn't".
